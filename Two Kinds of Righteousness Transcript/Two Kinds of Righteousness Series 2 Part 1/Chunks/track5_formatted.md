# Two Kinds of Righteousness Series 2 Part 1 - Track 5

**1 Corinthians 15:1-4**
[1] Moreover, brethren, I declare unto you the gospel which I preached unto you, which also ye have received, and wherein ye stand;
[2] By which also ye are saved, if ye keep in memory what I preached unto you, unless ye have believed in vain.
[3] For I delivered unto you first of all that which I also received, how that Christ died for our sins according to the scriptures;
[4] And that he was buried, and that he rose again the third day according to the scriptures:

The word "preached" means to announce something or to deliver a particular message. The core issue is that Christ died for our sins. This narrative should guide the reading of the Old Testament: it is Christ who died. If death is a symbolism of sin and the wrath of sin, and it is Christ who died, was buried, and raised from the dead, one must reread the Old Testament and rework their narratives. 

The phrase "according to the scriptures" (*kata tas graphas*) places the responsibility on the reader to read the whole book scholastically and academically, applying the rules of standard biblical studies. As previously discussed, the context of the Bible is primarily Genesis to Deuteronomy. The context of Deuteronomy is Numbers; Numbers is Leviticus; Leviticus is Exodus. Leviticus exists because there was a deliverance from Egypt. Numbers details the unbelief and the breakdown in worship and faith. Deuteronomy, the second law, exists because of that unbelief. The deliverance from Egypt occurred because of a promise made in Genesis. And the promise in Genesis was made because Genesis 1 and 2 reveal that something had broken down, and God wanted to restore it in his own man, made in the image of God. This defines reading the Bible "according to the scriptures."

The laws of Moses were modified legislation; there were already existing legislations in their day. For example, regarding Roman law and the concept of crucifixion, the Romans used a cross—two pieces of wood, one across the other. However, the Jews referred to a tree. 

**Galatians 3:13-14**
[13] Christ hath redeemed us from the curse of the law, being made a curse for us: for it is written, Cursed is every one that hangeth on a tree:
[14] That the blessing of Abraham might come on the Gentiles through Jesus Christ; that we might receive the promise of the Spirit through faith.

**1 Peter 2:24**
[24] Who his own self bare our sins in his own body on the tree, that we, being dead to sins, should live unto righteousness: by whose stripes ye were healed.

While "cross" is a Greek word, the Jewish writers specifically utilized the word "tree." The perspective—whether Jesus was crucified under Roman laws or the Jewish laws of blasphemy—dictates whether it is referred to as a tree or a cross.

These ancient societies had their own justice systems, and they were not of God. They executed malefactors and insurrectionists using their own laws, including practices such as crucifying individuals upside down. It is unjust and unfair to attribute these penal systems to God. Moses was modifying what was already in existence. The Hittite laws stated that sowing in someone else's land was punishable by death, whereas Moses modified this to require compensation. 

Ancient penal codes often lacked a concept of anger or wrath; it was strictly retribution. If someone mistakenly took another person's ox, the judge might order the person's death simply because the owner needed the ox at that moment. The laws tilted heavily in favor of the rich and against the poor. 

There is another concept that must be examined: "an eye for an eye."

**Exodus 21:23-25**
[23] And if any mischief follow, then thou shalt give life for life,
[24] Eye for eye, tooth for tooth, hand for hand, foot for foot,
[25] Burning for burning, wound for wound, stripe for stripe.

**Leviticus 24:17-21**
[17] And he that killeth any man shall surely be put to death.
[18] And he that killeth a beast shall make it good; beast for beast.
[19] And if a man cause a blemish in his neighbour; as he hath done, so shall it be done to him;
[20] Breach for breach, eye for eye, tooth for tooth: as he hath caused a blemish in a man, so shall it be done to him onward.
[21] And he that killeth a beast, he shall restore it: and he that killeth a man, he shall be put to death.

This is the law of compensation, known as *Lex Talionis* (retribution). It is often assumed that this mandates literal bodily mutilation. However, it is a figure of speech meant to ensure proportionate compensation. "Life for life" means if one kills, they will be killed—a concept that still exists today in capital punishment. "An eye for an eye" is not about physical mutilation; it is about weighing the offense and applying a penalty that fits the crime. It is a principle of fairness and justice.

Jesus explained this in Matthew 5.

**Matthew 5:38-42**
[38] Ye have heard that it hath been said, An eye for an eye, and a tooth for a tooth:
[39] But I say unto you, That ye resist not evil: but whosoever shall smite thee on thy right cheek, turn to him the other also.
[40] And if any man will sue thee at the law, and take away thy coat, let him have thy cloke also.
[41] And whosoever shall compel thee to go a mile, go with him twain.
[42] Give to him that asketh thee, and from him that would borrow of thee turn not thou away.

*Lex Talionis* is a principle that has been applicable for thousands of years. Bible historian Brevard Childs noted that Moses' legislation was an improvement from primitive law. It was meant to stop the rich and powerful from administering undue punishment. Without this regulation, a theft of an ox could result in the death of the thief, which would be "a life for an ox" rather than "an eye for an eye." Moses introduced these laws to reduce the severity of the primitive laws of their day. The Code of Hammurabi, for instance, mandated literal "bone for bone." 

There was a profound lack of regard for human life in these archaic codes. The Middle Assyrian laws (circa 1100 BC) featured extensive bodily mutilation. Moses attempted to instill a regard for human life by modifying such legislation. Before Moses, the laws were heavily oppressive and favored the wealthy. For example, if a rich man's ox killed someone, he might only be required to pay a negligible compensation, whereas a poor man might be put to death for a minor offense.


Moses placed a high premium on human lives. In Exodus 21:28-36, severe punishments are outlined for a lack of regard for life, such as an ox killing a person. Deuteronomy 24:16 mitigates archaic practices where children were killed for their parents' crimes, stating that a person shall not be put to death for the sins of their parents. Where older legislations mandated revenge, Moses differentiated between accidental killings (manslaughter) and intentional ones (murder). The law began to consider intent. 

**Exodus 21:12-14**
[12] He that smiteth a man, so that he die, shall be surely put to death.
[13] And if a man lie not in wait, but God deliver him into his hand; then I will appoint thee a place whither he shall flee.
[14] But if a man come presumptuously upon his neighbour, to slay him with guile; thou shalt take him from mine altar, that he may die.

**Exodus 21:28-30**
[28] If an ox gore a man or a woman, that they die: then the ox shall be surely stoned, and his flesh shall not be eaten; but the owner of the ox shall be quit.
[29] But if the ox were wont to push with his horn in time past, and it hath been testified to his owner, and he hath not kept him in, but that he hath killed a man or a woman; the ox shall be stoned, and his owner also shall be put to death.
[30] If there be laid on him a sum of money, then he shall give for the ransom of his life whatsoever is laid upon him.

These laws show a clear mitigation of manslaughter, offering the payment of a ransom rather than immediate death if the killing was not intentional.

**Numbers 35:30-31**
[30] Whoso killeth any person, the murderer shall be put to death by the mouth of witnesses: but one witness shall not testify against any person to cause him to die.
[31] Moreover ye shall take no satisfaction for the life of a murderer, which is guilty of death: but he shall be surely put to death.

Requiring multiple witnesses mitigated jungle justice and revenge. Thus, Moses implemented less strict laws. Historians agree that the laws of Moses were the mildest in the known world at that time. The allowance of a ransom for manslaughter speaks volumes. We must see the laws of Moses as modified legislation. Just as modern nations adopt and modify existing constitutions, Israel adapted its laws. These laws did not originate directly from the Spirit; they were modifications of existing archaic laws, designed as a schoolmaster. 

To properly understand the criminal justice system of the Old Testament, one must compare it to the legislation of their day to see what it was attempting to correct and step down from. 

When observing the wars in the Old Testament, including what we might call genocide today, one must consider the historical context. In modern warfare, armies operate with the intent to clear their enemies. When Israel fought the Amalekites and other nations, they were nations at war. This was the reality of their day, though it was not God's ideal plan. Even today, with a New Testament understanding of human rights and the knowledge that "the weapons of our warfare are not carnal" (2 Corinthians 10:4), some believers still pray for their enemies to "fall and die," applying the law of sin and death. 

When God instructed Israel to clear the land of idol worshipers, the people applied the only method they knew: the penal system of their day. God, in his sovereignty, took responsibility for actions he did not orchestrate. This illuminates the profound nature of the statement, "Christ died for our sins according to the scriptures." It means looking back at the scriptures and understanding that the ultimate victim of the law of sin and death was God in Christ. The laws of Moses were a modified criminal justice system. 

Another aspect of ancient practices was infant or child sacrifice. God never desired human sacrifice. When Abraham was willing to sacrifice Isaac, God stopped him, demonstrating that he did not want people to sacrifice their children. Yet, some erroneously teach that human sacrifice could satisfy the wrath of God.


In understanding these contradictions, early teachings such as the permissive-causative theory served as a helpful step. However, with deeper scholastic study, clearer facts emerge. Materialistic preaching often lifts scriptures entirely out of context to manipulate congregations. For instance, preachers may challenge believers to drop their "Isaac" in the offering bag. In context, Abraham took Isaac back because God provided a substitute. Context, implication, and application must always align.

This applies directly to the issue of infant and child sacrifice. 

**Leviticus 18:21**
[21] And thou shalt not let any of thy seed pass through the fire to Molech, neither shalt thou profane the name of thy God: I am the LORD.

**Leviticus 20:2-5**
[2] Again, thou shalt say to the children of Israel, Whosoever he be of the children of Israel, or of the strangers that sojourn in Israel, that giveth any of his seed unto Molech; he shall surely be put to death: the people of the land shall stone him with stones.
[3] And I will set my face against that man, and will cut him off from among his people; because he hath given of his seed unto Molech, to defile my sanctuary, and to profane my holy name.
[4] And if the people of the land do any ways hide their eyes from the man, when he giveth of his seed unto Molech, and kill him not:
[5] Then I will set my face against that man, and against his family, and will cut him off, and all that go a whoring after him, to commit whoredom with Molech, from among their people.

God explicitly abhorred child sacrifice. The theory that God gave Jesus to satisfy His own wrath severely undermines this truth. Child sacrifice was the ultimate abomination of idol worship.

**Deuteronomy 12:31**
[31] Thou shalt not do so unto the LORD thy God: for every abomination to the LORD, which he hateth, have they done unto their gods; for even their sons and their daughters they have burnt in the fire to their gods.

**Deuteronomy 18:10**
[10] There shall not be found among you any one that maketh his son or his daughter to pass through the fire, or that useth divination, or an observer of times, or an enchanter, or a witch,

Despite these explicit commandments, there are narratives of child sacrifice in the Old Testament that are frequently misunderstood.

**2 Kings 3:27**
[27] Then he took his eldest son that should have reigned in his stead, and offered him for a burnt offering upon the wall. And there was great indignation against Israel: and they departed from him, and returned to their own land.

This passage is often erroneously preached as an example of God's wrath being satisfied by a human sacrifice. However, the king of Moab was participating in idol worship, not sacrificing to God. The word used for "indignation" or "wrath" is *qetseph*, which is a cognate used for man-made wrath (as seen in 2 Kings 13:19). The sacrifice was to idols, not God.

Other kings of Israel and Judah fell into this same idolatrous abomination:

**2 Kings 16:3** (Regarding King Ahaz)
[3] But he walked in the way of the kings of Israel, yea, and made his son to pass through the fire, according to the abominations of the heathen, whom the LORD cast out from before the children of Israel.

**2 Kings 21:6** (Regarding King Manasseh)
[6] And he made his son pass through the fire, and observed times, and used enchantments, and dealt with familiar spirits and wizards: he wrought much wickedness in the sight of the LORD, to provoke him to anger.

This leads to the narrative of Jephthah in Judges 11.

**Judges 11:30-31**
[30] And Jephthah vowed a vow unto the LORD, and said, If thou shalt without fail deliver the children of Ammon into mine hands,
[31] Then it shall be, that whatsoever cometh forth of the doors of my house to meet me, when I return in peace from the children of Ammon, shall surely be the LORD's, and I will offer it up for a burnt offering.

When Jephthah returned, his only daughter met him, and verse 39 states that "he did with her according to his vow which he had vowed." While some laud this as a great commitment to God, they fail to understand the implication. In jurisprudence, there is a distinction between what *ought to be* and what actually *obtains*. The scriptures are honest historical records; they report what obtained. The fact that something is written in Scripture, or that the characters invoked God's name, does not mean their actions were right or endorsed by God.

The Bible honestly records the lack of understanding, unbelief, and rebellion of its figures. The Spirit of God came upon Samson (Judges 14:6), yet he touched a dead carcass (Judges 14:8-9) and visited a harlot (Judges 16:1). Gideon, an anointed judge (Judges 6:34), requested golden earrings and made an ephod that became an idol (Judges 8:24-27). Even David and Solomon, major figures, had profound moral and idolatrous failings.

The recording of these events is not a seal of approval. Just as Peter wielding a sword to cut off an ear, or James and John desiring to call down fire from heaven, were actions done by followers of Jesus, they were not endorsed by Him. The Bible is an honest book that reveals the profound need for a Savior. 

**Judges 17:6**
[6] In those days there was no king in Israel, but every man did that which was right in his own eyes.

**Judges 21:25**
[25] In those days there was no king in Israel: every man did that which was right in his own eyes.

These verses provide the context for the book of Judges. The people, including leaders like Jephthah, were deeply influenced by Canaanite practices. Did God ask Jephthah for a child? No. Was it an idol practice? Yes. Did Jephthah claim he vowed to God? Yes. Merely mentioning that God did something or that something was done for God does not make it divine truth. Jesus is the ultimate standard who corrects these misrepresentations of God.

There is also confusion regarding the offering of the firstborn. 

**Exodus 22:29**
[29] Thou shalt not delay to offer the first of thy ripe fruits, and of thy liquors: the firstborn of thy sons shalt thou give unto me.

Without proper reading, one might assume this commands human sacrifice.


Regarding the offering of the firstborn, one must examine the context. 

**Exodus 13:13**
[13] And every firstling of an ass thou shalt redeem with a lamb; and if thou wilt not redeem it, then thou shalt break his neck: and all the firstborn of man among thy children shalt thou redeem.

**Exodus 22:29-30**
[29] Thou shalt not delay to offer the first of thy ripe fruits, and of thy liquors: the firstborn of thy sons shalt thou give unto me.
[30] Likewise shalt thou do with thine oxen, and with thy sheep: seven days it shall be with his dam; on the eighth day thou shalt give it me.

This is not a command to offer human beings as sacrifices. It is an offering to commemorate what God did for them in Egypt, where the firstborn of Israel were spared (Exodus 4:23). The firstborn of man was to be redeemed. It was a worshipful acknowledgment of their deliverance. 

While infant sacrifice was a common practice in surrounding nations (2 Kings 16:3, 2 Kings 17:17, 2 Kings 23:10, 2 Chronicles 33:6), its prevalence does not mean God accepted it. That an action was common and God's name was mentioned does not legitimize it. The firstborn offering was historically specific, commemorating the deliverance from Egypt. To apply it today as a form of financial taxation or offering is taking the text completely out of context. 

Another text often misunderstood is Ezekiel 20. 

**Ezekiel 20:25-26**
[25] Wherefore I gave them also statutes that were not good, and judgments whereby they should not live;
[26] And I polluted them in their own gifts, in that they caused to pass through the fire all that openeth the womb, that I might make them desolate, to the end that they might know that I am the LORD.

Without context, this appears as though God gave bad laws intending for them to die and offer their children in the fire. However, the preceding verses provide the contrast.

**Ezekiel 20:11-13**
[11] And I gave them my statutes, and shewed them my judgments, which if a man do, he shall even live in them.
[12] Moreover also I gave them my sabbaths, to be a sign between me and them, that they might know that I am the LORD that sanctify them.
[13] But the house of Israel rebelled against me in the wilderness: they walked not in my statutes, and they despised my judgments, which if a man do, he shall even live in them; and my sabbaths they greatly polluted: then I said, I would pour out my fury upon them in the wilderness, to consume them.

God first gave them good statutes so they would live. Israel rebelled. In verse 13, the Hebrew word for statutes is *chuqqot* (feminine). In verse 25, the word used for the "statutes that were not good" is *chuqqim* (masculine). This shift in terminology indicates a figure of speech. Saying "I gave them statutes that were not good" is a sarcastic way of saying, "I allowed you to follow your own destructive laws." Similarly, in Romans 1, Paul states that God gave them over to a reprobate mind. This does not mean God actively handed them over, but that He did nothing to stop them from pursuing their own desires. 

This sarcastic tone is evident elsewhere in scripture.

**Ezekiel 20:39**
[39] As for you, O house of Israel, thus saith the Lord GOD; Go ye, serve ye every one his idols, and hereafter also, if ye will not hearken unto me: but pollute ye my holy name no more with your gifts, and with your idols.

**Amos 4:4**
[4] Come to Beth-el, and transgress; at Gilgal multiply transgression; and bring your sacrifices every morning, and your tithes after three years:

**1 Kings 22:15**
[15] So he came to the king. And the king said unto him, Micaiah, shall we go against Ramoth-gilead to battle, or shall we forbear? And he answered him, Go, and prosper: for the LORD shall deliver it into the hand of the king.

These are sarcastic statements meant to highlight rebellion. Mentioning God's name or saying "God said" requires the reader to think critically and read the entire narrative. The practice of picking isolated scriptures (often referred to as a "rhema" word) without understanding the context leads to significant theological error. 

Did God endorse slavery? No. Did God, through Moses, regulate slavery? Yes. God did not start the criminal justice system; men created it. Sin entered the world, and God intervened incrementally. Jesus brought the ultimate correction, instructing believers to turn the other cheek and love their neighbors. Misogyny, chauvinism, slavery, domination, and even human government were not present at the beginning. The biblical ideal of government is a theocracy, not the anarchy seen in Judges ("every man did that which was right in his own eyes"). 

The proper theological context of scripture must always remain:
1. Genesis 1 and 2 (The Ideal)
2. Genesis 3 (The Consequence of Sin)
3. Genesis 4 (The First Manifestation of Sin)

If this foundation is not solid, one will misread the entire Bible. The historical context of their day is also essential. For example, regarding slavery, Paul writes:

**1 Corinthians 7:20-21**
[20] Let every man abide in the same calling wherein he was called.
[21] Art thou called being a servant? care not for it: but if thou mayest be made free, use it rather.

Paul acknowledges the existence of slavery and instructs slaves to obtain their freedom if possible. The presence of an instruction regarding slavery does not mean God endorses the institution. God, in His grace, navigated through human weaknesses, cruelties, and archaic laws. The fact that a practice is recorded in scripture does not equate to divine endorsement. Human systems, including wealth and career structures, are inherently flawed and often built on oppression. We must view these narratives through the lens of God's overarching redemptive plan, culminating in Jesus Christ.


In the world, wealth and power are frequently built on foundations of exploitation. Therefore, one must be cautious not to glorify human systems or achievements as divine endorsements. 

Continuing with Paul's instruction:

**1 Corinthians 7:22-23**
[22] For he that is called in the Lord, being a servant, is the Lord's freeman: likewise also he that is called, being free, is Christ's servant.
[23] Ye are bought with a price; be not ye the servants of men.

Paul states that one's societal status has nothing to do with their identity in Christ. In the world, you are who you are, but if you can improve your situation and free yourself from servitude, do it. This involves using wisdom, diligence, and industry. However, one must always remember that even if they attain the status of a master, they remain the Lord's servant. Conversely, if one is a servant, they are free in Christ. There is no direct correlation between one's societal status in the world and their spiritual identity in Christ. 

God has not endorsed these oppressive human systems; rather, He has navigated through them, providing redemptive truth and the ultimate freedom found only in Christ.
