**QUESTION 1**

**Do an exegesis on the term gospel as done within this track.**

**ANSWER** 

***Matthew 28:18-20***

***\[18\] And Jesus came and spake unto them, saying, All power is given unto me in heaven and in earth.***

***\[19\] Go ye therefore, and teach all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost:***

***\[20\] Teaching them to observe all things whatsoever I have commanded you: and, lo, I am with you alway, even unto the end of the world. Amen.***

The word "commandment" in verse 20 is the Greek ***enthole*** (***entholomai***), which refers to instruction and doctrine, not merely rules. This word takes the mind directly to the books of the Old Testament, because the instructions Jesus refers to are rooted there. The word "teach" in verse 19 is the Greek ***matheteuo***, which means to train as a disciple. The Great Commission is therefore a mandate to train people through systematic teaching \- not to merely give them information, but to develop them into disciples with the ability to think and interpret correctly.

One of the primary things for which disciples are trained is exegesis. Exegesis is a skill \- the ability to ask good and intelligent interpretative questions about what one is reading, with the aim of arriving at correct understanding. There is a great difference between intelligent, interpretative questions and foolish questions. Paul instructs Timothy directly on this distinction.

***2 Timothy 2:23***

***\[23\] But foolish and unlearned questions avoid, knowing that they do gender strife.***

There are foolish questions that waste time and produce strife rather than understanding. There are also tempting questions \- questions asked not to learn but to trap. The Pharisees came to Jesus with such questions (Matthew 19:3). These are not the questions of exegesis. Exegesis demands questions that are deliberate and motivated by the genuine desire to interpret what is written. Two examples illustrate this well.

***Luke 2:46-47***

***\[46\] And it came to pass, that after three days they found him in the temple, sitting in the midst of the doctors, both hearing them, and asking them questions.***

***\[47\] And all that heard him were astonished at his understanding and answers.***

The word "asking questions" here is the Greek ***eperotao***, meaning to lay a request \- to make a petition to know. It is the posture of one genuinely seeking to understand. Jesus' questions were intelligent enough that the answers he gave in return astonished the doctors of the law. This is what exegesis looks like: precise questions that produce precise answers.

***Acts 17:11***

***\[11\] These were more noble than those in Thessalonica, in that they received the word with all readiness of mind, and searched the scriptures daily, whether those things were so.***

The Bereans searched the scriptures to see whether what Paul was teaching was accurate. That searching is exegesis. It is not passive reception; it is active, trained, interpretative engagement with the text. To do an exegesis on the term gospel is therefore to ask the precise questions that will uncover what this word actually means from within the world in which it was written, going back to its roots in the Hebrew and Greek languages and tracing it through the Old Testament, which is the background from which the New Testament draws its entire vocabulary.

Before that exegesis can proceed, the method by which scripture is to be interpreted must be established. Jesus demonstrated this method in Luke 24:

***Luke 24:25-27***

***\[25\] Then he said unto them, O fools, and slow of heart to believe all that the prophets have spoken:***

***\[26\] Ought not Christ to have suffered these things, and to enter into his glory?***

***\[27\] And beginning at Moses and all the prophets, he expounded unto them in all the scriptures the things concerning himself.***

***Luke 24:44-45***

***\[44\] And he said unto them, These are the words which I spake unto you, while I was yet with you, that all things must be fulfilled, which were written in the law of Moses, and in the prophets, and in the psalms, concerning me.***

***\[45\] Then opened he their understanding, that they might understand the scriptures,***

The word expounded in verse 27 is the Greek dihermeneuo, which means to give meaning through context and grammar. Jesus begins at Moses and the prophets. He mentions the prophets twice before he uses the word scriptures, in verse 25 and in verse 44\. This sequence is deliberate. Prophets in biblical narratives are those who interpret supernatural events, personalities, and activities. The Hebrew word is Nabi, from the root Naba, meaning to speak forth. The Greek is prophetes, from pro, meaning forth, and phemi, meaning to speak. When Jesus anchors his interpretation of the scriptures in the prophets, he is establishing that all scripture must be read within its supernatural context. Words in scripture do not carry meaning in themselves; meaning arises from the context and grammar in which a word is used. The understanding that verse 45 says was opened to the disciples, the Greek syniemi, comes from recognising the recurring patterns, or motifs, that run throughout the scriptures, and making connections through identification unifiers. That is how correct interpretation works. With that method in place, the word gospel can be properly examined.

**BODY**

**The meaning of the word gospel**

The word gospel appears in Mark 16:15 in the great commission:

***Mark 16:15***

***\[15\] And he said unto them, Go ye into all the world, and preach the gospel to every creature.***

The Greek word is Euaggelion. Jesus taught from the Septuagint, which is the Greek translation of the Hebrew Old Testament, abbreviated as LXX, meaning the translation made by seventy scholars. This means that when Jesus used the Greek word Euaggelion, he was not coining a new term with a new meaning. He was drawing on a Hebrew word that the Septuagint translates into Greek, and the root of that word must be found in the Old Testament. The Hebrew word is Basar. Basar is a word used for a kingdom and its news. It refers to the announcement of what is happening in the kingdom of God. Gospel is therefore not simply good news in a generic sense. It is kingdom information. To preach the gospel is to preach the kingdom. This understanding transforms everything that follows.

The connection between the gospel and the kingdom is confirmed in Isaiah 52:7, which is the classic Old Testament text on the word Basar:

***Isaiah 52:7***

***\[7\] How beautiful upon the mountains are the feet of him that bringeth good tidings, that publisheth peace; that bringeth good tidings of good, that publisheth salvation; that saith unto Zion, Thy God reigneth\!***

The phrase bringeth good tidings is the Hebrew Basar, and the announcement it carries is Thy God reigneth. To bring good tidings in this sense is to announce the reign of God. The gospel is the announcement of the reign of God. Isaiah 40 through 61 carries this Basar tradition throughout, and it is the background from which Jesus draws the word when he sends his disciples out to preach.

The gospel as Euaggelion and Basar is itself an exegetical word. It requires the trained skill of exegesis to understand it correctly, because it carries within it the entire Old Testament background of God's kingdom purposes. One of the disciplines of Bible study is to mark the connection wherever the word gospel appears, whether in Mark 16:15 or Matthew 28, and trace it back to Isaiah 52:7 and the Basar tradition that runs from Genesis through the Psalms and the prophets.

**The gospel in Genesis: the kingdom theme established**

To understand the word gospel correctly, the kingdom theme that it announces must be traced through the Old Testament from the beginning. The gospel as kingdom news begins in Genesis 1:

***Genesis 1:26, 28***

***\[26\] And God said, Let us make man in our image, after our likeness: and let them have dominion over the fish of the sea, and over the fowl of the air, and over the cattle, and over all the earth, and over every creeping thing that creepeth upon the earth.***

***\[28\] And God blessed them, and God said unto them, Be fruitful, and multiply, and replenish the earth, and subdue it: and have dominion over the fish of the sea, and over the fowl of the air, and over every living thing that moveth upon the earth.***

The words subdue and dominion are kingdom language. The image of God in man, the Hebrew Tselem meaning representative or idol, places man as God's agent of royal authority in the earth. The kingdom of God is established through man from the very beginning. This is the starting point of the gospel theme, the announcement of the reign of God through his representative in the earth.

Genesis 3:15 gives the first specific announcement of the coming king who will establish that kingdom in the face of opposition:

***Genesis 3:15***

***\[15\] And I will put enmity between thee and the woman, and between thy seed and her seed; it shall bruise thy head, and thou shalt bruise his heel.***

The word bruise here is the Hebrew rush. The seed of the woman is the one who will crush the head of the serpent. This is kingdom confrontation language. It is the first announcement of the coming king and his kingdom victory over the enemy. The gospel has its root here, in the promise of Genesis 3:15, in the announcement of the one whose coming is the coming of God's reign in the earth.

**The gospel through Abraham: kingdom through a chosen lineage**

The kingdom promise is then carried forward through the line of Abraham. In Genesis 17:6, God says to Abraham:

***Genesis 17:6***

***\[6\] And I will make thee exceeding fruitful, and I will make nations of thee, and kings shall come out of thee.***

Kings shall come out of thee. This is the continuation of the kingdom promise. The seed of the woman from Genesis 3:15 will come through the lineage of Abraham. The gospel, as kingdom news, is therefore being announced specifically through the line of Abraham. Paul confirms this directly in Galatians 3:8:

***Galatians 3:6-8***

***\[6\] Even as Abraham believed God, and it was accounted to him for righteousness.***

***\[7\] Know ye therefore that they which are of faith, the same are the children of Abraham.***

***\[8\] And the scripture, foreseeing that God would justify the heathen through faith, preached before the gospel unto Abraham, saying, In thee shall all nations be blessed.***

Paul says the scripture preached before the gospel unto Abraham. The word Basar, kingdom news, was announced to Abraham in Genesis. The gospel has its roots in Genesis, and it was preached to Abraham specifically. Paul's audience is reading Genesis. The children of Abraham in verse 7 is genealogy language that goes directly to Genesis 3:15 and the seed of the woman, and onward through the seed of Abraham. The seed, which is the Hebrew Zera and the Greek Sperma, is both singular and plural, just as it was in Genesis 3:15. Paul makes this explicit in Galatians 3:16:

***Galatians 3:16***

***\[16\] Now to Abraham and his seed were the promises made. He saith not, And to seeds, as of many; but as of one, And to thy seed, which is Christ.***

The seed is both collective, all who are in Christ, and singular, Christ himself. This is the eminent polar used by all Old Testament authors. Jesus embodies Israel, the singular seed who embodies the collective, as Matthew shows in Matthew 2:15 when he quotes Hosea 11:1, originally spoken about Israel, and applies it to Jesus coming out of Egypt.

**The gospel through Jacob, Judah, and the royal sceptre**

The kingdom promise narrows further through the line of Abraham. In Genesis 49:9-10, Jacob speaks over his sons and makes a declaration over Judah:

***Genesis 49:9-10***

***\[9\] Judah is a lion's whelp: from the prey, my son, thou art gone up: he stooped down, he couched as a lion, and as an old lion; who shall rouse him up?***

***\[10\] The sceptre shall not depart from Judah, nor a lawgiver from between his feet, until Shiloh come; and unto him shall the gathering of the people be.***

The sceptre is the symbol of royal authority. It will not depart from Judah until Shiloh comes. Shiloh is the one to whom the gathering of the people belongs, and he is the one in whom the royal authority rests in its fullness. This prophecy ties the kingdom promise directly to the tribe of Judah, the same tribe that will produce David, and through David, the Messiah. The announcement of the reign of God, the Basar, the gospel, is being channelled through a specific royal lineage.

In Exodus 19:5-6, the kingdom language becomes explicit when God speaks to Israel at Sinai:

***Exodus 19:5-6***

***\[5\] Now therefore, if ye will obey my voice indeed, and keep my covenant, then ye shall be a peculiar treasure unto me above all people: for all the earth is mine:***

***\[6\] And ye shall be unto me a kingdom of priests, and an holy nation. These are the words which thou shalt speak unto the children of Israel.***

A kingdom of priests, a peculiar treasure. The nation of Israel is to embody the kingdom of God in the earth. This is the vocation given to them at Sinai, the same kingdom theme that was given to Abraham, which originates in Genesis 1\. The gospel as kingdom news is the announcement of this kingdom, the announcement that God reigns, and that he reigns through his king and through his people.

**The gospel through David and the prophets**

The Basar tradition reaches its most developed form through David and the writing prophets. David is of the lineage of Judah and Abraham, the genealogy that leads to the Messiah. The word Basar appears in the Psalms in the context of David's kingdom. Zion and Jerusalem are David's language, as established in 2 Samuel 5 and 7\. Isaiah 40 through 61 carry the announcement of the kingdom forward most fully. Isaiah 52:7 has already been quoted, but the entire section from Isaiah 40 through 61 is the Basar tradition in full voice, the proclamation of the kingdom that was promised through the lineage of Genesis 3:15, Genesis 17:6, Genesis 49:10, and Exodus 19:6.

Ezekiel 34 calls the coming Messiah by the name David my servant, not referring to the historical David but to the one whose throne David's throne was a shadow of. This is the motif that runs through the prophets: the historical David points forward to the one in whom the kingdom is fully established. The gospel therefore carries within it the entire weight of this history. When Jesus says to his disciples in Mark 16:15, go and preach the gospel, that single word gospel compresses the full trajectory from Genesis 1 through David through Isaiah through Ezekiel, and the disciples who understood the exegetical tradition behind it would have understood immediately that they were being sent to announce the reign of God.

**The gospel in its fullness: Matthew 3 and the declaration at the Jordan**

Matthew 3:16-17 records the moment at which the gospel is announced in its fullest New Testament form, as God himself declares who the coming king is:

***Matthew 3:16-17***

***\[16\] And Jesus, when he was baptized, went up straightway out of the water: and, lo, the heavens were opened unto him, and he saw the Spirit of God descending like a dove, and lighting upon him:***

***\[17\] And lo a voice from heaven, saying, This is my beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased.***

This is my beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased draws from Psalm 2:7, from Isaiah 42:1, and from Genesis 22:2. The servant of Isaiah 42, the son of Psalm 2, all that was promised and planned through those texts, is identified here in Jesus. This is how the gospel tradition reaches its fulfillment. The word Basar, the kingdom news announced from Genesis 3:15 through Abraham, through Judah, through David, through Isaiah and Ezekiel, is now declared to have arrived in the person of this man. He is the seed of the woman. He is the seed of Abraham in whom all nations shall be blessed. He is the one in whom the sceptre of Judah rests. He is David's son. He is the servant and the son.

Matthew 17:5 repeats and extends this declaration at the transfiguration:

***Matthew 17:5***

***\[5\] While he yet spake, behold, a bright cloud overshadowed them: and behold a voice out of the cloud, which said, This is my beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased; hear ye him.***

Hear ye him. This is the fulfillment of Deuteronomy 18:15 and 18 and Jeremiah 18:15: the LORD thy God will raise up unto thee a Prophet from the midst of thee, him shall ye hear. Jesus is that prophet like Moses. Failure to hear the prophet would bring destruction. The gospel is therefore not only kingdom news; it is the word of the prophet who was to come, and hearing it is the appointed way of life.

**The gospel and the signs that follow**

Mark 16:15-20 establishes the connection between the gospel and the supernatural signs that follow its proclamation:

***Mark 16:15-20***

***\[15\] And he said unto them, Go ye into all the world, and preach the gospel to every creature.***

***\[16\] He that believeth and is baptized shall be saved; but he that believeth not shall be damned.***

***\[17\] And these signs shall follow them that believe; In my name shall they cast out devils; they shall speak with new tongues;***

***\[18\] They shall take up serpents; and if they drink any deadly thing, it shall not hurt them; they shall lay hands on the sick, and they shall recover.***

***\[19\] So then after the Lord had spoken unto them, he was received up into heaven, and sat on the right hand of God.***

***\[20\] And they went forth, and preached every where, the Lord working with them, and confirming the word with signs following. Amen.***

The word signs here is the Greek Semeion and the Hebrew Oth, meaning to point. Signs point beyond themselves to the reality they indicate. Because the gospel is supernatural in its nature, announcing the reign of a supernatural king and a supernatural kingdom, the signs that follow the preaching of that gospel are supernatural. Casting out demons, speaking with tongues, healing the sick through the laying on of hands: all these signs point to the supernatural kingdom being proclaimed. The signs confirm the word. They are not incidental additions to the gospel; they are the natural accompaniment of kingdom news when it is preached in the supernatural context out of which it arises.

**CONCLUSION**

The exegesis of the term gospel begins by establishing that the Greek Euaggelion draws its meaning from the Hebrew Basar, and that Basar means kingdom news, the announcement of what is happening in the kingdom of God. This is not a generic announcement of good news. It is a specific report on the reign of God, announced from the very beginning of scripture.

The kingdom theme that the gospel announces is first established in Genesis 1:26-28, where man is made as God's Tselem, his representative and agent of royal authority in the earth. It is carried forward as a promise through the seed of the woman in Genesis 3:15, through the royal lineage of Abraham in Genesis 17:6, through the royal sceptre of Judah in Genesis 49:9-10, through the kingdom of priests at Sinai in Exodus 19:5-6, through the reign of David and the Psalms, and through the full voice of the Basar tradition in Isaiah 40 through 61 and Ezekiel 34\. Paul confirms in Galatians 3:8 that the gospel was preached to Abraham, that the word Basar, kingdom news, was announced to him in Genesis. The gospel has its roots in the first books of Moses.

When Jesus commands his disciples in Mark 16:15 to go and preach the gospel, the entire weight of that history stands behind the word. And when Paul says in Galatians 3:13-14 that Christ has redeemed from the curse of the law so that the blessing of Abraham might come on the Gentiles through Jesus Christ, that is the fulfillment of the kingdom news first announced in Genesis 3:15 and continued through every stage of the Old Testament story. The gospel is the announcement of the reign of God, the arrival of the seed of the woman, the fulfillment of the promise made to Abraham, the enthronement of the one in whom the sceptre of Judah rests forever.

**QUESTION 2**

**Explain the interrelationship between the Land God promised Abraham, the gospel, and the spirit. How did the writers of the New Testament explain walking in the spirit from the Old Testament books of the Bible?**

**ANSWER** 

**INTRODUCTION**

There is something that happens to a reader of Paul when they encounter the promise of the Spirit in Galatians 3:14. Without the background of the Old Testament, the word Spirit in that verse sounds like a New Testament concept being introduced for the first time. But Paul is not introducing anything new. He is interpreting something old. He is doing what Jesus did in Luke 24: beginning at Moses and the prophets and expounding the things concerning the Spirit from within those books. The connection Paul draws is not one he invented; it is one that was always there, waiting to be read correctly.

The method by which scripture is to be interpreted must first be established, because without it the connection between the land, the gospel, and the Spirit cannot be seen. In Luke 24:25-27 and 44-45:

***Luke 24:25-27***

***\[25\] Then he said unto them, O fools, and slow of heart to believe all that the prophets have spoken:***

***\[26\] Ought not Christ to have suffered these things, and to enter into his glory?***

***\[27\] And beginning at Moses and all the prophets, he expounded unto them in all the scriptures the things concerning himself.***

***Luke 24:44-45***

***\[44\] And he said unto them, These are the words which I spake unto you, while I was yet with you, that all things must be fulfilled, which were written in the law of Moses, and in the prophets, and in the psalms, concerning me.***

***\[45\] Then opened he their understanding, that they might understand the scriptures,***

The word expounded is the Greek dihermeneuo, meaning to give meaning through context and grammar. Jesus anchors interpretation in Moses and the prophets. The understanding that verse 45 says was opened to the disciples, the Greek syniemi, comes from recognising the recurring motifs, the recurring patterns, that run through the scriptures, and making connections through identification unifiers. The Bible is a book that uses codified language, in which a word or phrase in the New Testament compresses and points back to an entire narrative in the Old Testament. The land, the gospel, and the Spirit are one such set of codified connections. To see them as separate is to have read the surface of the text without entering the world in which it was written.

One must also note the nature of the prophets whose writings form the background of these connections. Prophets are earthly messengers of supernatural realities. The Hebrew Nabi, from Naba, means to speak forth, and the Greek prophetes carries the same meaning. When Paul writes about the land, the gospel, and the Spirit, he is interpreting the prophets in the same way Jesus instructed his disciples to interpret them: from within the supernatural context in which they wrote.

**BODY**

**The land God gave Abraham**

The promise of land first comes to Abraham in Genesis 12:1:

***Genesis 12:1, 4***

***\[1\] Now the LORD had said unto Abram, Get thee out of thy country, and from thy kindred, and from thy father's house, unto a land that I will shew thee:***

***\[4\] So Abram departed, as the LORD had spoken unto him...***

The word get thee out is the Hebrew alak, which means to go, to journey, to proceed. This is a walk. God's first instruction to Abraham is to walk: to go out toward the land God would show him. The walk of Abraham is therefore the first model for what walking with God looks like in the Old Testament. He departed. He went on a journey. He walked toward the land God gave him.

In Genesis 13:14-17, after Lot has separated from Abraham, God speaks again:

***Genesis 13:14-17***

***\[14\] And the LORD said unto Abram, after that Lot was separated from him, Lift up now thine eyes, and look from the place where thou art northward, and southward, and eastward, and westward:***

***\[15\] For all the land which thou seest, to thee will I give it, and to thy seed for ever.***

***\[17\] Arise, walk through the land in the length of it and in the breadth of it; for I will give it unto thee.***

Arise, walk through the land. The same word alak. God gives Abraham a land to walk to and a land to walk through. Moses says God gave Abraham a land. Paul says God gave Abraham a promise. These are not two different things. They are the same thing described in two different registers. Moses uses land because that is the language Abraham understood. Paul unpacks the spiritual reality that land was pointing to. The land is codified language for a spiritual reality that Paul names as the Spirit.

**Paul's exegesis: the land is the Spirit**

The connection that Paul draws between the land and the Spirit is not a reinterpretation of Moses. It is the exegesis of Moses. Paul says in Galatians 3:13-14:

***Galatians 3:13-14***

***\[13\] Christ hath redeemed us from the curse of the law, being made a curse for us: for it is written, Cursed is every one that hangeth on a tree:***

***\[14\] That the blessing of Abraham might come on the Gentiles through Jesus Christ; that we might receive the promise of the Spirit through faith.***

Redemption here is drawn from the Genesis 15 context, where God told Abraham that his descendants would be in bondage in a foreign land for four hundred years, and that he would deliver and redeem them. That deliverance through Moses in Exodus 3 through 6 is the outworking of that promise. Paul says Christ has redeemed from the curse of the law so that the blessing of Abraham might come on the Gentiles through Jesus Christ. And then he names what the blessing of Abraham is. He does not say the promise of the land. He says the promise of the Spirit. The land that God asked Abraham to walk through is what Paul calls the promise of the Spirit. To walk in the land and to walk in the Spirit are one and the same reality.

This is consistent with how the entire book of Galatians is constructed. Galatians is an exposition of Genesis and Exodus. In Galatians 4:21, Paul calls Genesis the law: Tell me, ye that desire to be under the law, do ye not hear the law? And then he proceeds to quote Genesis 16:15 and 21:2, the account of Hagar and Sarah and their sons. By calling Genesis the law, Paul confirms that the word nomos is a multilateral term. It does not refer only to the Mosaic legislation. It can mean the law of bondage, the law of life in Christ, the law of sin and death as in Romans 8:2, and the law of the promise. In Galatians 6:2, the law of Christ is not a new legal code; it is the law of the promise, the law by which Abraham walked. The walk through the land and the walk in the Spirit are the same walk.

**The land as a picture of worship: the walk as the tabernacle**

The relationship between the land, the gospel, and the Spirit becomes even clearer when the word walk, the Hebrew alak, is traced through the Old Testament from its earliest appearances. In Genesis 5:22-24:

***Genesis 5:22-24***

***\[22\] And Enoch walked with God after he begat Methuselah three hundred years, and begat sons and daughters:***

***\[24\] And Enoch walked with God: and he was not; for God took him.***

***Genesis 6:9***

***\[9\] These are the generations of Noah: Noah was a just man and perfect in his generations, and Noah walked with God.***

Enoch and Noah both walked with God. The same word alak. These are not descriptions of geographical movement. They describe the spiritual posture of men whose lives were oriented toward God, men who went through life in step with God. The word that describes Abraham's walk to and through the land of Canaan is the same word that describes the spiritual walk of Enoch and Noah with God. To walk in the land is to walk with God. To walk with God is to walk in the Spirit. Paul draws on exactly this pattern when he writes in Galatians 5:16: Walk in the Spirit.

The deepest root of the walk is in Genesis 3:8:

***Genesis 3:8***

***\[8\] And they heard the voice of the LORD God walking in the garden in the cool of the day: and Adam and his wife hid themselves from the presence of the LORD God amongst the trees of the garden.***

The word walking is again alak. God himself is walking in the garden. The garden, the Hebrew gan, means a protected, secure, enclosed place. The garden of Eden is not merely an agricultural space. It is a sacred place where God dwelt and walked, a picture of the tabernacle. God built the garden and brought man to co-dwell with him there. This is God tabernacling among men. The walk of God in the garden is therefore tabernacle language. When Adam and Eve sinned, they were sent out from the presence of the LORD, out of Eden in Genesis 3:23 and 4:16 to the east of Eden. The departure from the garden is a departure from the sacred space where God walked among men. The entire redemptive story is the story of God restoring that walk, of God returning to tabernacle with his people.

Leviticus 26:11-12 shows God renewing the Edenic pattern at Sinai:

***Leviticus 26:11-12***

***\[11\] And I will set my tabernacle among you: and my soul shall not abhor you.***

***\[12\] And I will walk among you, and will be your God, and ye shall be my people.***

I will set my tabernacle among you. I will walk among you. This is the Edenic pattern restored through the covenant with Israel. The walk of God is the tabernacling of God among men. The same word used in Genesis 3:8 of God walking in the garden appears here of God walking among Israel. Deuteronomy 23:14 extends this further:

***Deuteronomy 23:14***

***\[14\] For the LORD thy God walketh in the midst of thy camp, to deliver thee, and to give up thine enemies before thee; therefore shall thy camp be holy: that he see no unclean thing in thee, and turn away from thee.***

The LORD walks in the midst of the camp. The camp of Israel in the wilderness was structured around the tabernacle at its centre, and God walks through it. The walk of God in the camp is the same pattern as the walk of God in the garden. The land is not separate from this tabernacle language. The land is the place where God walks with his people. To walk through the land is to walk in God's presence. To walk in God's presence is to walk in the Spirit.

In 2 Kings 5, when Naaman was healed, he asked Elisha if he could take dirt from the place where he stood, to take it back with him to worship on. The land was the place of worship, the sacred ground where God was encountered. Jesus makes this connection explicit with the woman at the well in John 4, where he says that God seeks those who will worship him in spirit and in truth. God is a Spirit. The land that was the place of worship Jesus interprets as Spirit. The walk through the land is the walk in the Spirit.

**Paul applies the walk to the New Testament church: 2 Corinthians 6**

Paul draws these strands together explicitly in 2 Corinthians 6:14-16:

***2 Corinthians 6:14-16***

***\[14\] Be ye not unequally yoked together with unbelievers: for what fellowship hath righteousness with unrighteousness? and what communion hath light with darkness?***

***\[16\] And what agreement hath the temple of God with idols? for ye are the temple of the living God; as God hath said, I will dwell in them, and walk in them; and I will be their God, and they shall be my people.***

Paul quotes Leviticus 26:11-12 and applies it to the church of Jesus Christ. The believers are the temple of the living God. God will dwell in them and walk in them. The same walk that God walked in the garden with Adam and Eve, the same walk he promised to walk among Israel in the wilderness, is now fulfilled in the church through the Holy Spirit. The tabernacle of the wilderness has become the body of the believer. To walk in the Spirit is therefore to be the tabernacle through which God walks in the earth. This is not a New Testament innovation; it is the fulfillment of the pattern that was established in Genesis 3:8.

**The fruit of the Spirit as the fruit of the land**

Paul's use of the word fruit in Galatians 5:22 is not an accident of vocabulary. The Greek word is karpos, and Paul uses it because the text his audience is reading speaks about land. In the land, one expects fruit. The fruit of the Spirit is the fruit of living in and walking through the land that God gave to Abraham, which is the Spirit. To those reading Paul from the Old Testament background, fruit of the Spirit would immediately connect to the produce of the land they were walking in.

Galatians 5:1 establishes the context:

***Galatians 5:1, 13-16***

***\[1\] Stand fast therefore in the liberty wherewith Christ hath made us free, and be not entangled again with the yoke of bondage.***

***\[13\] For, brethren, ye have been called unto liberty; only use not liberty for an occasion to the flesh, but by love serve one another.***

***\[14\] For all the law is fulfilled in one word, even in this; Thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself.***

***\[15\] But if ye bite and devour one another, take heed that ye be not consumed one of another.***

***\[16\] This I say then, Walk in the Spirit, and ye shall not fulfil the lust of the flesh.***

The liberty to which believers are called is the Sabbath rest of the redeemed. It is not a licence for the flesh; it is the rest of those who have been delivered from Egypt, who are now walking in the land God gave them. The warning in verse 15 is precise: if you bite and devour one another, you will be consumed. Strife within the body of Christ does not merely create social problems. It deprives everyone involved of the ability to minister to one another and to receive. Strife and gossip are not merely character flaws; they are weapons against the walk of the Spirit in the community. The fruit of the Spirit, listed in Galatians 5:22-23, is the natural outcome of walking in the land:

***Galatians 5:22-23***

***\[22\] But the fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, longsuffering, gentleness, goodness, faith,***

***\[23\] Meekness, temperance: against such there is no law.***

Against the fruit of the Spirit there is no law. This confirms the connection to the land. The one who walks in the Spirit and produces its fruit is already living in the fullness of what the law was pointing toward. The law was the instruction for how to walk in the land. The Spirit is the land. The fruit of the Spirit is the natural produce of living in and walking through the land that God gave Abraham, which Paul names as the promise of the Spirit.

**The gospel as the announcement of the walk**

The interrelationship between the land, the gospel, and the Spirit is now visible in its completeness. The gospel is the Basar, the kingdom news. It is the announcement of the reign of God. The land is the place where God's reign is expressed and where his presence walks among his people. The Spirit is what the land always was in its deepest reality: the sacred space of God's presence, the place where God walks with man. Paul does not say the promise of the land when quoting the Abraham narrative. He says the promise of the Spirit in Galatians 3:14. This is not a contradiction of Moses. It is the exegesis of Moses. What Moses called land, Paul calls Spirit. The land is the codified language by which God communicated to Abraham a spiritual reality in terms he could understand. God communicates through language that his hearers can grasp. Abraham understood land, so God used land to describe the spiritual reality that Paul unpacks as the Spirit.

The gospel is therefore the announcement of the arrival of the one through whom the promise of the land, which is the promise of the Spirit, is fulfilled. In Galatians 4:4-7:

***Galatians 4:4-7***

***\[4\] But when the fulness of the time was come, God sent forth his Son, made of a woman, made under the law,***

***\[5\] To redeem them that were under the law, that we might receive the adoption of sons.***

***\[6\] And because ye are sons, God hath sent forth the Spirit of his Son into your hearts, crying, Abba, Father.***

***\[7\] Wherefore thou art no more a servant, but a son; and if a son, then an heir of God through Christ.***

Made of a woman goes to Genesis 3:15. Made under the law goes to Abraham through Moses. To redeem them under the law goes to Exodus 6:6-8. The adoption of sons, the Greek huiothesia, is God's adoption of Israel as his son, which is drawn from Exodus 4:22-23 where God says Israel is my son, my firstborn, the Hebrew Beni Bekori. The Spirit of his Son crying Abba, Father is the Spirit now living in the believer, continuing the cry of the redeemed son. All of this is Genesis into Exodus. The promise of the Spirit is the fulfillment of everything the land promised.

**The Ten Words as instructions for the walk**

The Ten Commandments are commonly misunderstood as requirements for achieving salvation. Exodus 20:1-3 corrects this misunderstanding precisely:

***Exodus 20:1-3***

***\[1\] And God spake all these words, saying,***

***\[2\] I am the LORD thy God, which have brought thee out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of bondage.***

***\[3\] Thou shalt have no other gods before me.***

The saving act precedes the instructions. God redeems first, then gives the commandments. The Ten Commandments are instructions for those who are already saved, for those who are already walking in the land. In the oldest tradition, they are not called commandments at all; they are called Davar, ten words, as confirmed in Exodus 34:28 and Deuteronomy 4:13. They are written as a continuous statement, not ten separate numbered rules but ten words that flow as a single unbroken declaration, consistent with the format of ancient Near Eastern covenant treaties. What God is saying through the ten words is: I am this. You reflect me. When you do these things, you are reflecting who I am in the earth. They are instructions for the walk in the land. They are instructions for walking in the Spirit. This is why Paul is able to write in Romans 13 that love fulfils the law:

***Romans 13:8-10***

***\[8\] Owe no man any thing, but to love one another: for he that loveth another hath fulfilled the law.***

***\[9\] For this, Thou shalt not commit adultery, Thou shalt not kill, Thou shalt not steal, Thou shalt not bear false witness, Thou shalt not covet; and if there be any other commandment, it is briefly comprehended in this saying, namely, Thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself.***

***\[10\] Love worketh no ill to his neighbour: therefore love is the fulfilling of the law.***

When Paul says love fulfils the law, he is drawing on the same connection that Jesus makes in Matthew 22:36-40, where Jesus quotes from Deuteronomy 6:4-5 and Leviticus 19:18 and says that all the law and the prophets hang on these two commandments. And in John 13:34, Jesus says:

***John 13:34***

***\[34\] A new commandment I give unto you, That ye love one another; as I have loved you, that ye also love one another.***

The word new here does not mean a replacement. It means renewed. Jesus is repeating and renewing the commandment. Love one another as I have loved you. The word commandment here is entolay. When Jesus says in Matthew 28:20 to observe, the Greek tereo, all things that he commanded, he is referring to the entolay of love that fulfils the entire law. The fruit of the Spirit in Galatians 5:22-23, which begins with love, is therefore the natural outcome of the walk in the Spirit, the walk through the land, the walk in the presence of God. The connection is complete. The land, the gospel, and the Spirit are one continuous reality described from three different angles within the one body of scripture.

**CONCLUSION**

The interrelationship between the land God promised Abraham, the gospel, and the Spirit is not a theological inference drawn from isolated texts. It is the consistent exegetical pattern that runs through the entire body of scripture from Genesis to the Epistles, visible to those who have been trained to read the Old Testament as the background and context of the New.

The land is the sacred space where God walks with his people. God walked in the garden with Adam and Eve in Genesis 3:8. He promised to walk among Israel in Leviticus 26:12 and Deuteronomy 23:14. He walked Abraham through Canaan in Genesis 13:17. Paul shows in Galatians 3:14 that what Moses called land is what Paul calls the Spirit. The land was always codified language for the spiritual reality of God's presence, the place where the walk of God with man takes place. Moses used land because Abraham understood land. Paul unpacks the spiritual reality because the Spirit has been poured out and the fullness of what the land pointed to has arrived.

The gospel is the announcement of the arrival of the one through whom this promise is fulfilled, the seed of the woman from Genesis 3:15, the seed of Abraham from Genesis 17:6, the son of David from Psalm 2, the servant of Isaiah 42\. When the gospel is preached, the kingdom is announced, and the Spirit is the substance of the kingdom. The fruit of the Spirit in Galatians 5:22-23 is the fruit of the land, the natural produce of walking in the presence of God, just as Abraham walked through Canaan, just as Enoch and Noah walked with God, and just as Paul commands the church to walk in Galatians 5:16: Walk in the Spirit. The walk is the same alak. The land is the same Spirit. The gospel is the announcement that the walk has been restored.
