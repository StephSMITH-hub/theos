**QUESTION 1**

**"Discipleship delivers to believers an attitude where they are able to think". Accept or refute this assertion based on your understanding from this track.**

**INTRODUCTION**

The local church is not primarily a place where people come to feel good. It is where a curriculum is followed. The distinction matters, because what a person receives in a place where a curriculum is consistently taught is qualitatively different from what they receive in a place that merely responds to the emotional temperature of the room. 2 Timothy 3:16-17 states:

***2 Timothy 3:16-17***

***\[16\] All scripture is given by inspiration of God, and is profitable for doctrine, for reproof, for correction, for instruction in righteousness:***

***\[17\] That the man of God may be perfect, throughly furnished unto every good work.***

The word perfect in verse 17 is the Greek artios, meaning complete and equipped. The man of God who is completely furnished is the one who has been comprehensively trained through the scriptures in all four functions Paul names: doctrine, reproof, correction, and instruction in righteousness. This kind of thoroughness cannot be produced by irregular exposure or by teaching that follows the preferences of the audience. It requires systematic, consistent, curriculum-based discipleship. And what that discipleship produces is not simply a body of knowledge. It produces an attitude.

Before the assertion can be fully accepted or refuted, the method by which scripture is to be interpreted must be established. Everything that follows in the teaching flows from the method Jesus himself demonstrated:

***Luke 24:25-27***

***\[25\] Then he said unto them, O fools, and slow of heart to believe all that the prophets have spoken:***

***\[26\] Ought not Christ to have suffered these things, and to enter into his glory?***

***\[27\] And beginning at Moses and all the prophets, he expounded unto them in all the scriptures the things concerning himself.***

***Luke 24:44-45***

***\[44\] And he said unto them, These are the words which I spake unto you, while I was yet with you, that all things must be fulfilled, which were written in the law of Moses, and in the prophets, and in the psalms, concerning me.***

***\[45\] Then opened he their understanding, that they might understand the scriptures,***

The word expounded in verse 27 is the Greek dihermeneuo, meaning to give meaning through context and grammar. The word understand in verse 45 is the Greek syniemi, meaning to reason or read together. Jesus mentions the prophets before he uses the word scriptures, and he does this twice, in verse 25 and in verse 44\. This establishes that all scripture must be read within its supernatural context, and that understanding is not a private achievement but a communal one, formed through the practice of reasoning through the text with others. These two words, dihermeneuo and syniemi, name the two skills that discipleship develops.

**BODY**

**The assertion accepted: discipleship forms an attitude, not merely information**

The assertion is accepted, and the teaching establishes this clearly. The key word is attitude. The Greek word in Matthew 28:19 for making disciples is mathiteuo, which means to train as a disciple. This is not the same as recruiting or enrolling. To make disciples is to impart the message and one's lifestyle into them. 1 Thessalonians 2:8 describes what Paul did with the Thessalonians:

***1 Thessalonians 2:87***

***\[8\] So being affectionately desirous of you, we were willing to have imparted unto you, not the gospel of God only, but also our own souls, because ye were dear unto us.***

Paul imparted not only the gospel but his own soul, his very life. That is what discipleship looks like: message and lifestyle together. And when message and lifestyle are consistently imparted together over time, what forms in the recipient is not a collection of facts but a way of seeing, a way of thinking, a way of approaching any text or situation. That is the attitude the assertion refers to. Skills, by their nature, become second nature. A person who has been consistently trained in exegetical thinking does not wait to be shown how to interpret a text. They begin asking the right questions automatically, because the attitude has been formed.

The teaching contrasts this with the opposite condition, using two examples from scripture. The first is the disciples on the road to Emmaus, who are described in Luke 24:25 as slow of heart to believe all that the prophets have spoken. The slowness Jesus identifies is not emotional dullness. It is interpretative dullness, a failure of the thinking that correct discipleship would have formed. Their approach to the scriptures lacked the sharpness that should have been there. The second example is the Galatians:

***Galatians 3:1***

***\[1\] O foolish Galatians, who hath bewitched you, that ye should not obey the truth, before whose eyes Jesus Christ hath been evidently set forth, crucified among you?***

The Galatians were so soon removed from the grace of Christ to another gospel in Galatians 1:6-7. They had allowed different influences to shape their thinking in place of consistent discipleship. The word foolish Paul uses is the Greek anoetos, meaning without understanding, unthinking. The condition of the Galatians was not an absence of religious activity. It was a failure of interpretative thinking, the very thing correct discipleship would have preserved.

**What discipleship produces: the skill of exegesis**

The primary intellectual skill that discipleship forms is exegesis, the ability to ask good and intelligent interpretative questions when reading the scriptures, questions that lead to correct understanding. Paul calls this diligence in 2 Timothy 2:15:

***2 Timothy 2:15***

***\[15\] Study to shew thyself approved unto God, a workman that needeth not to be ashamed, rightly dividing the word of truth.***

The word study is to be diligent, spoudazo in Greek, meaning to hasten, to be earnest, to give urgent attention. The workman who rightly divides the word of truth is not an occasional reader. The dividing of the word of truth, the Greek orthotomeo, means to cut straight, to handle accurately. This is a craft skill, and craft skills are formed through sustained practice under consistent guidance. Diligence in the things of God, formed through years of consistent training, becomes an attitude, the very thing the assertion identifies.

The teaching also identifies the skill of reading, the Greek anaginosko, as another product of discipleship. Jesus repeatedly asks in the Gospels: have you not read? as in Matthew 12:3-5, 19:4, and 22:31. The word anaginosko includes the skill of explanation. To read in the biblical sense is not to pass the eyes over text but to engage with it interpretatively. Ephesians 3:4 shows Paul connecting reading with understanding:

***Ephesians 3:4***

***\[4\] Whereby, when ye read, ye may understand my knowledge in the mystery of Christ;***

Reading, when done by someone whose mind has been formed by consistent discipleship, produces understanding of the mystery of Christ. The same text read by someone whose mind has not been formed in this way may produce nothing at all. The difference is not in the text. The difference is in the attitude that has or has not been formed.

**Discipleship requires community and contagion**

A further dimension of the assertion is the communal nature of the attitude discipleship forms. The teaching states plainly that one cannot be discipled in isolation. It requires community and the consistent influence of someone who already carries the skill. This is the pattern Paul establishes in 2 Timothy 2:2:

***2 Timothy 2:2***

***\[2\] And the things that thou hast heard of me among many witnesses, the same commit thou to faithful men, who shall be able to teach others also.***

The things that have been heard must be committed to faithful men who will teach others. The skill is transmitted from person to person. The interpretative thinking of the teacher is absorbed by the disciple through consistent exposure, the way a student of music absorbs the phrasing and style of the master through sustained listening. The teaching calls this contagion. Skills are contagious. When a person is consistently in a local assembly where the scriptures are opened, quoted, and read at depth, an interpretative culture forms. The ability to think through a text becomes second nature, not only in the person who teaches but in every person who has been immersed in the culture long enough.

This is why the local assembly is the irreplaceable context for the formation of this attitude. The local church is where the curriculum is followed, where the scriptures are opened with depth, and where the habit of interpretative thinking is formed over time. The attitude described in the assertion is not the product of a retreat weekend or a seminar. It is the product of years of consistent, curriculum-based discipleship in community. Luke 24:45 uses syniemi, to reason together, precisely because the understanding that is opened is communal in nature. It is formed by people reading and reasoning through the text with one another under the guidance of one who already carries the interpretative skill.

**The attitude formed enables the believer to imitate correctly**

A further evidence that the assertion is correct is found in how the epistles use Old Testament characters as models. The attitude that discipleship forms is what equips a believer to imitate those models correctly. Hebrews 6:12 states:

***Hebrews 6:12***

***\[12\] That ye be not slothful, but followers of them who through faith and patience inherit the promises.***

The word followers is the Greek mimetes, imitators. Believers are called to imitate Old Testament characters who through faith and patience inherited the promises. This imitation is not naive copying. It requires the ability to read the Old Testament story, to understand what the character's conduct actually demonstrated, and to identify what should be imitated and what should not. Without the interpretative attitude formed by discipleship, a person cannot do this. They either reject the story as irrelevant because it predates the New Testament, or they imitate the wrong things because they have not been trained to read accurately. The attitude that discipleship forms is precisely the capacity to read these stories correctly and to know what they demand of the believer's conduct.

Paul models this in 1 Corinthians 4:16 and 11:1 where he says be followers of me. In Ephesians 5:1 he says be followers of God as dear children. In 1 Thessalonians 1:6 he records that the believers became followers of him and of the Lord. In each case, the imitation is of a person whose conduct has been formed by the same consistent discipleship and whose life therefore shows what walking in the Spirit looks like in practice. The chain of imitation, from God to Christ to apostle to believer, functions only where the attitude that discipleship forms is present. Without it, the chain breaks.

**CONCLUSION**

The assertion that discipleship delivers to believers an attitude where they are able to think is accepted fully and without qualification. The teaching establishes this on multiple grounds.

Discipleship, which is the process named by the Greek mathiteuo in Matthew 28:19, is the impartation of message and lifestyle together through consistent, curriculum-based training in community. It produces not merely information but a way of thinking: the interpretative attitude that enables a believer to approach any text with the right questions and arrive at correct understanding. The opposite of this attitude is what Jesus identifies in Luke 24:25 as slowness of heart, and what Paul identifies in Galatians 3:1 as the foolishness of the undiscipled, who are vulnerable to any influence that comes their way because no stable interpretative culture has been formed in them.

The skills formed by discipleship, exegesis through dihermeneuo, understanding through syniemi, reading through anaginosko, and diligence through spoudazo, are each attitudes before they are techniques. They are ways of approaching the scriptures that become second nature through consistent exposure to a teacher who already carries them. They are contagious within a community where the curriculum is consistently followed. And they are what equips the believer to imitate correctly, to read Old Testament characters as the models of conduct the epistles present them as being, and to walk in the Spirit with the full understanding of what that walk means. This is what discipleship delivers, and this is why it cannot be substituted or bypassed.

**QUESTION 2**

You have been called upon to write an article using the following scripture texts: Genesis 13:6-8 and Genesis 13:17. Offer your detailed explanation.

**INTRODUCTION**

The Old Testament is not background material to the New Testament. It is the foundational lecture from which the New Testament draws all of its content. Romans 15:4 states:

***Romans 15:4***

***\[4\] For whatsoever things were written aforetime were written for our learning, that we through patience and comfort of the scriptures might have hope.***

Whatsoever things were written aforetime, that is, every account and every instruction in the Old Testament, was written for learning. The stories of the patriarchs are not decorative. They are doctrinal. They are the evidence base for the theology the epistles express in condensed, codified form. When Paul speaks of walking in the Spirit in Galatians 5:16, or of the promise of the Spirit in Galatians 3:14, or of the children of Abraham in Galatians 3:7, he is drawing on narratives from Genesis that his audience was expected to know. Genesis 13 is one such narrative, and it carries within it two of the most important doctrinal realities for the life of the church: the doctrine of strife, and the doctrine of the walk in the Spirit.

The method by which these texts are to be read must first be established. Jesus demonstrated this in Luke 24:

***Luke 24:25-27***

***\[25\] Then he said unto them, O fools, and slow of heart to believe all that the prophets have spoken:***

***\[26\] Ought not Christ to have suffered these things, and to enter into his glory?***

***\[27\] And beginning at Moses and all the prophets, he expounded unto them in all the scriptures the things concerning himself.***

The word expounded is the Greek dihermeneuo, meaning to give meaning through context and grammar. Jesus begins at Moses. Genesis 13 is Moses. And Moses is the foundational text from which all subsequent biblical teaching on both strife and the walk in the Spirit draws its meaning.

**ARTICLE: The Land, the Brother, and the Walk**

**The situation in Genesis 13**

Genesis 13:6-8 reads:

***Genesis 13:6-8***

***\[6\] And the land was not able to bear them, that they might dwell together: for their substance was great, so that they could not dwell together.***

***\[7\] And there was a strife between the herdmen of Abram's cattle and the herdmen of Lot's cattle: and the Canaanite and the Perizzite dwelled then in the land.***

***\[8\] And Abram said unto Lot, Let there be no strife, I pray thee, between me and thee, and between my herdmen and thy herdmen; for we be brethren.***

The word strife in verse 7 is the Hebrew meribah, from the root rib, which means to quarrel, to dispute, to strive. This is the first occurrence of strife in scripture, and its first occurrence is not between enemies. It is between the servants of two men who are brethren. The detail in verse 7 is not incidental: the Canaanite and the Perizzite dwelled in the land. The surrounding peoples, those who do not belong to the covenant, are watching. The strife that arises among the brethren is visible to those outside the covenant. This is the first thing to understand about strife: it is never a private matter. It is always a public testimony about the nature of the community in which it occurs.

Abraham's response in verse 8 is the foundational doctrinal statement on strife: let there be no strife, I pray thee, between me and thee, and between my herdmen and thy herdmen; for we be brethren. Abraham elevated the bond of brotherhood above every commercial consideration. The substance of both men was great. The land could not bear them both. There was a real, material pressure that was the occasion of the strife. But Abraham did not negotiate the land on commercial terms or assert his seniority. He invoked the one thing that transcended the material dispute: we are brethren.

**The first occurrence of strife and what it establishes**

The first occurrence of a word or theme in scripture is always the foundational statement. Everything that follows it builds on what is established here. This is why Genesis 13:7-8 carries such doctrinal weight. The word meribah that first appears here reappears throughout the Old Testament at critical moments: in the waters of Meribah in the wilderness where Israel strove against Moses, which Numbers 27:14 records as a trespass against the Lord himself, in Psalm 95:8 where the hardening of the heart in the provocation is the same meribah, and in Psalm 106:32 where the anger at the waters of strife brought consequences on Moses. Every subsequent use of the word draws on this foundational occurrence.

The contrast the teaching establishes is between Genesis 13 and Genesis 4\. In Genesis 4, the word ach, brother, is present: and Cain rose up against Abel his brother, and slew him in verse 8, and where is Abel thy brother in verse 9\. In Genesis 4, the presence of brotherhood did not prevent strife from escalating to murder. In Genesis 13, the presence of brotherhood is the very argument Abraham uses to stop strife before it escalates. Genesis 13 is the corrective of Genesis 4\. Where Genesis 4 shows what strife produces when the brotherhood bond is ignored, Genesis 13 shows what faith does with the brotherhood bond: it elevates it above every material interest.

The sequence that produces strife is visible in Genesis 4:5-6. Cain's countenance fell. His face darkened with a sense that what he deserved had been denied him. That falling countenance is the beginning of rib. Wrath and a sense of superiority, the conviction that one must be right and the other must be wrong, produce the quarrel that escalates into strife. Strife therefore does not begin with an event. It begins with an attitude in the heart, the anger of verse 5, long before the murder of verse 8\. The warning of the teaching on strife is therefore: watch the falling countenance, watch the rising anger, watch the sense of grievance, because these are the early warning signs of the sin that begins in the heart before it becomes visible in conduct.

**Strife as a sin against God, not only against a brother**

What Numbers 27:14 establishes about the word meribah must not be missed. In the strife of the congregation at the waters of Meribah, the trespass was not recorded as a dispute between men. It was a trespass against the Lord:

***Numbers 27:14***

***\[14\] For ye rebelled against my commandment in the desert of Zin, in the strife of the congregation, to sanctify me at the water before their eyes: that is the water of Meribah in Kadesh in the wilderness of Zin.***

The strife looked like a dispute between the congregation and Moses. In God's reckoning, it was a sin against him. This is the gravity of strife. When it appears among the brethren, whether between herdmen, between families, between ministers, between members of a local assembly, it looks like a human conflict. But the meribah tradition in the Old Testament establishes that it is a sin against the Lord himself. Psalm 106:32 records that the anger at the waters of strife brought consequences on Moses. Moses was present in the conflict not as a principal aggressor but as the one through whom God was working. Strife against the man God is working through is strife against God.

This is why the epistles treat strife with such severity. In Galatians 5:20, strife is listed alongside idolatry and witchcraft as a work of the flesh. In 1 Corinthians 3:3, Paul tells the Corinthians that strife and divisions prove they are carnal, walking as men. The presence of strife in a community is evidence of the absence of the Spirit's governance. And because the meribah tradition shows that strife is a sin against God, not merely a social problem, the only appropriate response to it is the response Abraham modelled: to invoke the bond of brotherhood and release every material claim that is fuelling the dispute.

**Genesis 13:17 and the walk in the Spirit**

The same chapter that establishes the doctrine of strife also carries the foundational statement on the walk in the Spirit. After Abraham has released the land to Lot and the strife has been resolved, God speaks to him. Genesis 13:17:

***Genesis 13:17***

***\[17\] Arise, walk through the land in the length of it and in the breadth of it; for I will give it unto thee.***

The word walk is the Hebrew alak, meaning to go, to journey, to proceed. This is not an agricultural instruction to survey the boundaries of a property. The word alak is the same word used of Enoch in Genesis 5:22-24:

***Genesis 5:22-24***

***\[22\] And Enoch walked with God after he begat Methuselah three hundred years, and begat sons and daughters:***

***\[24\] And Enoch walked with God: and he was not; for God took him.***

And the same word used of Noah in Genesis 6:9:

***Genesis 6:9***

***\[9\] These are the generations of Noah: Noah was a just man and perfect in his generations, and Noah walked with God.***

Enoch walked with God. Noah walked with God. Both uses of alak describe a spiritual posture, a life oriented toward God, a movement through the world in step with God's purposes. The word that describes the spiritual walk of Enoch and Noah is the same word God uses when he tells Abraham to arise and walk through the land. The walk through the land is therefore a walk in the Spirit. The land is not primarily geographical. As Paul establishes in Galatians 3:14, what Moses calls the land is what Paul calls the promise of the Spirit. God gave Abraham a land; the New Testament writers identify it as the promise of the Spirit. The walk through the land is the walk in the Spirit.

**The connection between resolving strife and the walk in the Spirit**

It is not accidental that God's command to arise and walk in Genesis 13:17 comes immediately after the resolution of strife in Genesis 13:8. Abraham refused strife, released the land to Lot, and immediately after, God gave him the entire land. The order is instructive. The person who refuses strife and releases what they hold materially for the sake of the brotherhood bond is precisely the person who receives the fullness of what God promised. This is the walk in the Spirit: not hoarding, not asserting, not contending, but releasing, because the Spirit is given not to those who grasp but to those who walk in the freedom of the redeemed.

Galatians 5:15-16 places these two realities side by side:

***Galatians 5:15-16***

***\[15\] But if ye bite and devour one another, take heed that ye be not consumed one of another.***

***\[16\] This I say then, Walk in the Spirit, and ye shall not fulfil the lust of the flesh.***

The warning in verse 15 about biting and devouring is the meribah warning: strife among the brethren will consume the community. And the instruction that immediately follows in verse 16 is: walk in the Spirit. The connection is deliberate. The walk in the Spirit is the antidote to strife. And the foundational illustration of that walk, drawn from Genesis 13, is Abraham refusing strife and arising to walk through the land God gave him.

**The walk as the purpose of the church in the earth**

The teaching establishes that the walk God commanded Abraham to walk in Genesis 13:17 is not merely about Abraham as an individual. It is the pattern for the conduct of the entire church. The background for God's walk is tabernacle language. God walks among men when he tabernacles with them. This is established in Genesis 3:8, where God walks in the garden with alak, and is renewed in Leviticus 26:11-12:

***Leviticus 26:11-12***

***\[11\] And I will set my tabernacle among you: and my soul shall not abhor you.***

***\[12\] And I will walk among you, and will be your God, and ye shall be my people.***

And in 2 Corinthians 6:16:

***2 Corinthians 6:16***

***\[16\] And what agreement hath the temple of God with idols? for ye are the temple of the living God; as God hath said, I will dwell in them, and walk in them; and I will be their God, and they shall be my people.***

Paul applies Leviticus 26:11-12 to the church. The same walk that God walked in the garden, the same walk he promised to walk among Israel in the wilderness, is now fulfilled in the church through the Holy Spirit. When God tells Abraham to arise and walk through the land, he is saying: this is my tabernacle, act as my image in it. The walk of Abraham through the land is the pattern for the church's walk in the Spirit through the earth. Our conduct in the earth is not about earning a passage to another place. It is worship. It is the imaging of God. It is the extension of God's rest through the earth.

Isaiah 60:1 carries the same structure in a different textual setting:

***Isaiah 60:1***

***\[1\] Arise, shine; for thy light is come, and the glory of the LORD is risen upon thee.***

Arise and shine. Arise and walk. The same command in two different registers. The light of Isaiah 60:1 is the light of Genesis 1:3 spreading through the earth. The walk of Genesis 13:17 is the same light moving through the land God has given. Arise and walk in the land, arise and walk in the Spirit, arise and shine: these are the same instruction given to the people who are the temple of God, the resting place of God in the earth.

**CONCLUSION**

Genesis 13:6-8 and Genesis 13:17 carry two foundational doctrinal realities that the epistles draw on throughout. The first is the doctrine of strife. Strife in its first occurrence arises among brethren over a material dispute. Abraham's response establishes the definitive teaching: we are brethren, and the bond of brotherhood takes precedence over every material claim. The meribah tradition that follows this foundational occurrence across the Old Testament establishes that strife is not merely a social problem among men; it is a sin against God himself. The severity with which the epistles treat strife, listing it alongside idolatry in Galatians 5:20, is rooted in this foundational narrative.

The second reality is the walk in the Spirit. The command in Genesis 13:17, arise and walk through the land, uses the same Hebrew alak that describes Enoch and Noah walking with God, and that Paul identifies in Galatians 3:14 as the promise of the Spirit. The land God gave Abraham is what Paul calls the promise of the Spirit. To walk through the land is to walk in the Spirit. And the sequence in Genesis 13 is the pattern: first, refuse strife and release every material claim for the sake of brotherhood; then, arise and walk in the fullness of what God has given. This is the conduct of the church: free from strife, free from the flesh, walking through the land of God's promise, which is the Spirit, imaging God in the earth as his temple and resting place.

**QUESTION 3**

Itemize examples believers are not to follow from this teaching.

**INTRODUCTION**

The Old Testament provides not only positive models for believers to follow but also negative examples, personalities and patterns of conduct whose stories are preserved in scripture precisely so that the church may recognise and avoid them. 1 Corinthians 10:6 and 11 establishes the principle:

***1 Corinthians 10:6, 11***

***\[6\] Now these things were our examples, to the intent we should not lust after evil things, as they also lusted.***

***\[11\] Now all these things happened unto them for ensamples: and they are written for our admonition, upon whom the ends of the world are come.***

The word examples in verse 6 and ensamples in verse 11 is the Greek tupos, meaning a type or pattern. These things happened and were recorded for the specific purpose of admonition: so that those who read them would see the pattern clearly enough to refuse it in their own lives. The epistles draw on these negative examples through codified language, compressing the Old Testament narrative into a phrase or name that points back to the full story. To recognise the warning in the epistle, one must know the story. The following examples from this teaching are the patterns that believers are not to follow.

**BODY**

**The way of Cain: strife, hatred, and murder of a brother**

The first and most foundational negative example is Cain. Jude 11 names this directly:

***Jude 11***

***\[11\] Woe unto them\! for they have gone in the way of Cain, and ran greedily after the error of Balaam for reward, and perished in the gainsaying of Core.***

The way of Cain is strife that begins in a fallen countenance, rises through anger and the sense of superiority, and ends in the destruction of a brother. Genesis 4:2-9 records the full account:

***Genesis 4:2, 8-9***

***\[2\] And she again bare his brother Abel. And Abel was a keeper of sheep, but Cain was a tiller of the ground.***

***\[8\] And Cain talked with Abel his brother: and it came to pass, when they were in the field, that Cain rose up against Abel his brother, and slew him.***

***\[9\] And the LORD said unto Cain, Where is Abel thy brother? And he said, I know not: Am I my brother's keeper?***

The way of Cain is not to be followed. The dynamic that produced it, which is the wrath of verse 5 and the falling countenance of verse 6, is the early warning signal. The teaching identifies this sequence: a sense of superiority, then anger, then quarrelling, then strife. This sequence must be watched for and refused from its very earliest stage. When countenance falls and anger rises, one is already in the early motion of Cain's way. The contrast to follow is Abraham in Genesis 13:8, who refused strife before it could escalate by invoking the brotherhood bond and releasing every material claim.

**The error of Balaam: the use of spiritual gifts for financial gain**

The second negative example named in Jude 11 is Balaam. The error of Balaam is the ran greedily after reward. Balaam was a man with genuine prophetic function, but he used that function to negotiate for financial payment. The story from Numbers 22 through 24 and 31 records how Balaam was hired by Balak to curse Israel. When he was unable to curse them directly, he counselled Balak in how to draw Israel into sin. The error of Balaam is therefore the corruption of ministry through financial motivation.

The negative example is not primarily about money in the abstract. It is about the reduction of the sacred to a commodity, the treatment of the gifts and calling of God as a tool for personal financial advancement. This example is not to be followed. The believer and the minister who uses their gift primarily for financial reward, who makes ministry decisions based on what they will receive in return, who positions their service in the kingdom in relation to what they will gain, is in the error of Balaam. The model to follow is the opposite: ministry as service, not as commerce.

**The profaneness of Esau: treating sacred things as common**

The third negative example the teaching identifies is Esau. Hebrews 12:16-17 states:

***Hebrews 12:16-17***

***\[16\] Lest there be any fornicator, or profane person, as Esau, who for one morsel of meat sold his birthright.***

***\[17\] For ye know how that afterward, when he would have inherited the blessing, he was rejected: for he found no place of repentance, though he sought it carefully with tears.***

Esau is the example of the profane person. The word profane is the Greek bebelos, meaning trodden underfoot, common, unhallowed. Esau sold his birthright for a single meal. The birthright was a sacred inheritance, the standing before God and the covenant people that was his by right of birth. He traded it for bread and a pottage of lentils in Genesis 25:33-34. He treated as common what was sacred, as weightless what had eternal weight.

The example of Esau is not to be followed. The profane person is the one who gives what is sacred a low valuation, who treats the inheritance of the Spirit, the calling of God, the community of faith, or the word of God as less valuable than what the world is offering at the moment. Verse 17 records the consequence: he found no place of repentance, though he sought it carefully with tears. This is the gravity of profaneness. There are decisions that, once made, cannot be undone by subsequent regret. The pattern of Esau is therefore to be refused at the point of its first temptation, before the trade is made.

**The gainsaying of Core: rebellion against God's appointed man**

The fourth negative example named in Jude 11 is Core, the Greek form of Korah. The account is in Numbers 16\. Korah led a rebellion against Moses, the man God had appointed, claiming that all the congregation was holy and that Moses had taken too much upon himself. The gainsaying of Core is therefore the spirit of rebellion against the man through whom God is working, dressed as a concern for equality or congregational holiness. Numbers 16:31-33 records what followed: the earth opened and swallowed Korah and those who were with him.

The gainsaying of Core is not to be followed. The pattern to recognise is the following: the congregational reasoning that questions the authority of God's appointed man by appealing to a principle that sounds holy, the spirit that says all of us are equally holy, why do you set yourself above us? The teaching establishes consistently that God works through men, that the man God has appointed carries a ministry that must be honoured, and that opposing that ministry is opposing God himself. The murmuring against Moses that Psalm 106:32 records as bringing consequences was the congregation striving, the meribah of the wilderness, which Numbers 27:14 records as a sin against the Lord himself. The core of the gainsaying of Core is the refusal to honour God in the man he has chosen.

**The strife of the herdmen: allowing material disputes to create division among brethren**

From Genesis 13:7, the conduct of the herdmen is itself a pattern not to follow, though the primary teaching in that passage is Abraham's corrective response. The herdmen of Abram and the herdmen of Lot allowed a material pressure, the insufficiency of the land for both parties, to generate strife. The detail in verse 7 that the Canaanite and the Perizzite dwelled in the land establishes the public consequence: those outside the covenant see the strife of those within it.

The strife pattern in Genesis 13:7 is not to be followed. Material pressures within the community of believers, whether over resources, roles, influence, or recognition, are not occasions for strife. They are occasions for Abraham's response: we are brethren, and the brotherhood bond takes precedence over every material claim. The pattern to refuse is the pattern of allowing what the land cannot bear to become an argument between those who are ach, brothers, in the covenant. When that argument begins, the Canaanite and the Perizzite are watching.

**The slow and unresponsive heart: neglecting the training of interpretative thinking**

The final example the teaching identifies as something not to follow is not a named individual but an attitude: the slow and unresponsive heart that Jesus rebuked in Luke 24:25. The disciples on the road to Emmaus were slow of heart to believe all that the prophets had spoken. This is the condition of those who have not been formed by consistent discipleship, who have not developed the interpretative attitude that consistent exposure to sound teaching produces.

Paul makes the same charge against the Galatians in Galatians 3:1, calling them foolish, the Greek anoetos, without understanding. The Galatians had been so soon removed from the grace of Christ to another gospel in Galatians 1:6-7, not because the teaching they had received was insufficient but because they had not maintained the consistency of exposure that would have formed a stable interpretative culture in them. The slow heart, the anoetos mind, the condition of those who drift from sound teaching whenever a more appealing influence presents itself: this is not to be followed. The response is 2 Timothy 2:15:

***2 Timothy 2:15***

***\[15\] Study to shew thyself approved unto God, a workman that needeth not to be ashamed, rightly dividing the word of truth.***

Diligence in the things of God, consistent engagement with the curriculum of the local assembly, the sustained practice of asking good and intelligent interpretative questions of the text: this is the opposite of the slow and unresponsive heart. The example of the disciples on the road to Emmaus and the Galatian church are held before the believer as warnings: this is what happens when the formation of the interpretative attitude is neglected.

**CONCLUSION**

The teaching draws from the Old Testament and from the Epistles a set of negative examples that function as warnings for the church. The way of Cain is the pattern of strife that treats a brother as an enemy, beginning with a fallen countenance and rising through anger to destruction. The error of Balaam is the corruption of ministry through the motivation of financial reward, the reduction of the sacred to a commodity. The profaneness of Esau is the treating of what is sacred as common, the trading of eternal inheritance for immediate earthly gratification. The gainsaying of Core is the spirit of rebellion against the man God has appointed, dressed as congregational righteousness but rooted in the refusal to honour God in his chosen instrument.

To these is added the pattern of the herdmen in Genesis 13:7, who allowed a material pressure to generate strife between those who were brethren, making the conflict visible to those outside the covenant. And the pattern of the slow and unresponsive heart, the attitude of those who have neglected the consistent discipleship that would have formed in them the ability to think through the scriptures accurately and to resist every other gospel that presents itself.

All of these examples are preserved in scripture as ensamples, tupos, types written for the admonition of those upon whom the ends of the world are come. They are not incidental stories. They are the negative side of the doctrinal narration that the epistles carry in codified form, and they are as much a part of the curriculum of the local assembly as the positive models. The believer who knows these patterns is equipped to recognise them in their earliest stages and to refuse them before they escalate.
